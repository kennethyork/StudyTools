About StudyTools

Free Bible study tools that run entirely in your browser — no accounts, no tracking, no server.

What this is

StudyTools is a collection of small, dependency-free Bible study apps. Everything is static HTML, CSS and JavaScript, so the whole site can be served from any static host with no build step and no backend.

Between them they read the Bible in three modern public-domain translations, search it word by word, write notes against a verse, carry a plan through the whole text a day at a time, keep the church's year from the Book of Common Prayer (1928), and open any passage in the Greek or Hebrew behind it.

Notes, journals, memory decks, reading-plan ticks and study progress are saved in your browser's local storage and never leave your device.

It can be installed on a phone or a laptop and then used with no connection at all: a service worker keeps the pages you have visited, so the site opens on a plane. And because there is no account to hang your reading on, Your Data gathers everything it has saved in one place — a backup file to keep, or a short code to carry to another device, where the two copies are added together rather than one overwriting the other.

The companion site is Witness, a cited term base for interfaith translation: how each tradition renders a concept, and what each rendering loses. It runs with no server too.

The Bible texts

Seven translations ship with the site, all in the public domain, which is why they can be bundled, copied, printed and shared freely. Three are modern and carry the Apocrypha: the World English Bible (Updated), the modernized King James Version and the modernized Revised Version. Two keep to the sixty-six book canon: the American Standard Version (1901) and Young's Literal Translation (1862), both brought into present-day English by this project like the Revised Version. One is Jewish: the Jewish Publication Society's Tanakh of 1917, modernized, for the Old Testament — the same reading the Parallel Passages tool sets beside a Christian one. One is Catholic and older: the Douay-Rheims of 1752, which reads the Latin Vulgate and numbers it the Vulgate's way (see below).

The Parallel Passages tool quotes the Qur'an, the Jewish Tanakh and the Book of Mormon beside the Bible on the same figures and events. Only the passages it compares are bundled here — the full texts are deliberately not part of this site — and each is quoted from its own public-domain edition, modernized the same way as the Bibles above.

Note: the three modern texts are genuinely independent translations, not editions of one another. Measured against each other, under 2% of verses are word-for-word identical between WEBU and RVM, and about 12% between KJVM and RVM. All three of those carry the Apocrypha, though they present it differently: WEBU has the Greek Esther and Daniel, Psalm 151 and 3–4 Maccabees, while KJVM and RVM keep the additions to Daniel as separate books (Susanna, Bel and the Dragon, the Prayer of Azariah).

On numbering

The reader, Study a Passage and the verse panel keep every table that is keyed to the Hebrew numbering — cross-references, commentary, the interlinear, the Prayer Book's offices — and turn a Douay-Rheims reference into it before looking anything up. Its Psalm 22 shows what the commentators said about Psalm 23. The six Masoretic-numbered translations use the same chapter and verse numbering, so a reference lines up exactly between them. The Douay-Rheims does not: it keeps the Vulgate's, where Psalm 22 is the Hebrew Psalm 23, where Psalms 9 and 10 are one psalm and 114 and 115 are another, and where Daniel and Esther carry their additions as chapters. It is therefore read on its own — the reader says so on every chapter, names the Hebrew psalm you are actually in, and turns a shared link like Psalm 23 into the psalm the Douay-Rheims calls 22 — and it is kept out of the Verse Matrix, the verse panel and the study page, so that nothing is placed beside the wrong verse. That is the whole reason it is offered this way rather than dropped.

How the data is built and checked

The texts and tables the tools use are not typed in by hand. Each is built from its source by a script in scripts/, and the parts a reader would notice getting wrong are checked by scripts of their own, which anyone can run again:

  • The church year — Easter by the usual computus, every season derived from it, every date from 1900 to 2100 confirmed to have both a morning and an evening office, and every Sunday named as the Prayer Book names it · check-liturgical.js
  • The readings — the Prayer Book's printed references, alternatives and all, read back to book, chapter and verse · check-refs.js
  • The reading plans — every chapter of a plan read exactly once, in order, with no empty day and no day more than one chapter longer than another · check-plan.js
  • Search — the word index compared against the text it indexes, book by book and word by word, with the two tokenisers pinned to one another · check-search.js
  • Notes — the store rules, both orderings and the Markdown export · check-notes.js
  • Devotional verses — every quoted verse re-checked against the bundled text, so the site cannot print a misquotation · verify-devotional.py
  • Concordance — the Strong's index re-checked against the interlinear it was built from, word by word: every verse a word is listed in has to contain it, and every verse it appears in has to be listed · check-concordance.js
  • Modernized translations — each one re-checked against the text it was made from: the structure verse for verse, the archaic forms, and every word, so a rule cannot invent one · verify-modernized.py
  • Commentary — the verses and introductions it is indexed to, and the books it has nothing for · check-commentary.js
  • Ask the site — every question answered out of the files above, and the links each answer offers · check-answer.js
  • The atlas — the box the basemap was cropped to against the box the page draws it at, the raster's own shape against the degrees it claims, the ground against the window the map opens on, the name sizes in the stylesheet against the ones the collision boxes are measured with, no name drawn twice, and every verse a place is named in against the text in the reader · check-atlas.js
  • The pages — what each one loads and says about itself, including a stylesheet and its own button styling · check-pages.js
  • The offline copy — the service worker driven against a stubbed network: pages and scripts fetched past the browser's cache so a deploy is not left invisible for ten minutes, data revalidated, everything falling back to the cache with no connection, and every file the shell installs actually shipped · check-sw.js

Note: the commentary is public domain and uneven, and the panel says so where it is. The Gospels carry the church fathers as well: Aquinas' Catena Aurea, in the Oxford translation of 1842, on Matthew and Mark verse by verse, each remark attributed to the Father who made it. (CCEL holds no text volume for Luke or John, so those two have none.) The three main works cover the sixty-six; the deuterocanonical books are Haydock's — his Catholic Bible Commentary (1859) goes through Tobit, Judith, the Greek Esther, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, the Epistle of Jeremiah, Susanna, Bel and the Dragon and the Maccabees verse by verse, and his introduction to each of them is shown as about this book. Six books are outside his range, and for four of them — 1 and 2 Esdras, 3 and 4 Maccabees — the panel shows the article on the book from Hastings' Dictionary of the Bible (1909), which this site already bundles, under a line saying that is what it is. The Prayer of Manasses is covered by R. H. Charles (1913) — his introduction to it, transcribed from a scan of the edition, and disclosed in the panel as a transcription rather than commentary. One book has nothing at all: Psalm 151, which no public-domain work here treats as a book, and the panel says so. Because these commentators attach a remark to the verse that opens a passage, most individual verses have none — that is the shape of the material, not a fault in the reader. They also print front matter before most chapters, and that is carried too, as the chapter's introduction: 1,178 of them, which is where Jamieson, Fausset & Brown comment on Genesis 1:1, and the whole of what they wrote on the Song of Solomon. The three works are Protestant in range, so no deuterocanonical book has any commentary at all.

Where a check has found something wrong, the fix went into the data or the arithmetic rather than into a footnote: an Epiphany taking its year from Christmas, a Sunday Lectionary three weeks out because a year has fewer Sundays after the Epiphany than the tables allow for, and a lexicon lookup that left everyday Hebrew words blank.

Privacy

Nothing is uploaded. There are no accounts, no cookies, no analytics and no server to talk to. Everything you type — verse notes and highlights, journal entries, memory decks, reading-plan ticks, saved ideas — stays in your browser's local storage, and clearing your browser data removes it.

One thing can leave your device, and only if you ask for it. The reader can offer a small language model to ask about a verse. It runs on your own machine through WebGPU, so nothing you read or type is sent anywhere — but the model itself is downloaded once, from Hugging Face (via WebLLM), and kept in your browser's cache until you clear it. It is off until you turn it on, it tells you the download size before it starts, and it cannot verify anything it says.

Following the link to Witness, the companion site, takes you off this one — that site will see the visit, as any website does. Nothing here sends it anything.
